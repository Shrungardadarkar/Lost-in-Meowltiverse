# Behavioral design: recreational flow charter

## Outcome

Design for a player who finishes a short session feeling capable, curious, calmer, and free to stop. Engagement is a consequence of satisfying play, never the product metric by itself.

The framework uses flow research on perceived challenge–skill balance, clear goals, feedback, attention, and control; and self-determination theory’s needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness. See the sources at the end of this file.

## Design loop

| Moment | Player need | Game response | Evidence to collect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Orient | Know what matters now | One visible goal and one readable world rule | Can a new player state the goal? |
| Practice | Build competence safely | A low-cost first use of the mechanic | Do players understand the cause of a bounce? |
| Commit | Choose a meaningful challenge | An optional risk or route with a clear payoff | Do players choose, rather than stumble into it? |
| Recover | Stay willing to learn after failure | Clear cause, rapid retry, saved elevation | Do players retry without confusion or self-blame? |
| Reflect | Integrate what happened | Short story beat or a freed spirit’s response | Can players describe what changed? |
| Close | Leave satisfied | Biome rest, pause, or clear end state | Do players feel able to stop? |

## Rules that apply to every design

### Autonomy

- Offer optional portals, paths, and play pace. Mark the likely consequence before the commitment.
- Preserve pause and safe exit at all times. Never punish a player for leaving.
- Do not use streaks, timed re-entry gifts, limited events, notification pressure, daily chores, or manipulative scarcity.

### Competence

- Teach one new demand at a time: preview → low-risk trial → mastery shot → release.
- Keep collision, flipper response, gravity, and failure cause legible.
- Use deterministic rewards for explicit actions. A room may hide a discovery, but its reward conditions must be learnable and its outcome cannot be a lottery.
- Do not gate story, recovery, or basic progression behind score.

### Relatedness and care

- Treat the dog as a hopeful trace rather than an anxiety timer.
- When freeing a possessed cat, transform tension into relief, connection, and a small truthful clue.
- Do not make the player feel responsible for an endless world emergency.

### Sensory load

- The active physics rule must be encoded in at least two stable channels: geometry, motion, color, sound, or text.
- Psychedelic effects may decorate the safe comprehension layer; they cannot replace it.
- Avoid flashes, strobe-like luminance shifts, uncontrolled camera movement, continuous alerts, and impact effects that hide the ball or flippers.
- Supply reduced-motion alternatives that preserve the gameplay cue through color, shape, and sound.

## Anti-compulsion boundaries

Do not add variable-ratio rewards, loot boxes, uncertain rare drops, gambling-adjacent audiovisual celebration, paid recovery, artificial waiting, near-miss presentation, leaderboard pressure, autoplay, or infinite escalation without a documented owner decision and an independent ethical review. A random layout can support replay only when every room remains understandable, escapable, and fairly recoverable.

## Feature review card

Before approval, write concise answers:

1. What skill is being practiced?
2. What does the player choose?
3. What is the explicit goal and immediate feedback?
4. Which flow demand changes, and how does it match likely skill?
5. How does the player recover from a mistake?
6. What stops the feature from becoming a compulsion loop?
7. What cue remains when reduced motion is enabled?
8. Where can the player stop satisfied?
